Basics of logic and sets

Chapter 0

Before talking about points, lines and triangles we need to agree on the language. Mathematics is written in English (or in any other language), but with a handful of words used with a far more precise meaning than in everyday conversation: and, or, not, if…then, for all, there exists. This chapter gathers the minimum of logic and set theory we need in order to read and write proofs. It is not meant to be a course in logic; it is a toolbox.

§0.1

Propositions and connectives

Definition 0.1.1 · Proposition

§0.1 Propositions and connectives
Definition 0.1.1 · Proposition

A proposition is a statement of which it makes sense to say that it is true or that it is false, but not both at once. Its truth value is T\mathrm{T}T (true) or F\FF (false).

Example 0.1.2

§0.1 Propositions and connectives
Example 0.1.2

The following are propositions:

  1. “777 is a prime number” (true);

  2. “2+2=52+2=52+2=5” (false);

  3. “every even number greater than 222 is the sum of two primes” (it is a proposition: it is either true or false, even though to this day nobody knows which; it is Goldbach’s conjecture).

The following are not propositions: “what time is it?”, “solve the equation”, or “x+1=5x+1=5x+1=5”. The last one becomes a proposition when we give xxx a value or when we quantify it.

Propositions and connectives

§0.1 Propositions and connectives

Propositions are usually denoted by lowercase letters p,q,r,…p,q,r,\ldotsp,q,r,… From simple propositions we build more complex ones by means of logical connectives.

Definition 0.1.3 · Logical connectives

§0.1 Propositions and connectives
Definition 0.1.3 · Logical connectives

Let ppp and qqq be propositions.

  1. The negation ¬p\no p¬p (“not ppp”) is true when ppp is false, and false when ppp is true.

  2. The conjunction p∧qp\y qp∧q (“ppp and qqq”) is true only when both are true.

  3. The disjunction p∨qp\ou qp∨q (“ppp or qqq”) is false only when both are false.

  4. The conditional p⟹qp\entonces qp⟹q (“if ppp, then qqq”) is false only when ppp is true and qqq is false.

  5. The biconditional p⟺qp\sii qp⟺q (“ppp if and only if qqq”) is true when ppp and qqq have the same truth value.

The mathematical “or” is inclusive

§0.1 Propositions and connectives
The mathematical “or” is inclusive

In everyday life “would you like soup or rice?” usually means “one of the two, not both”. In mathematics, p∨qp\ou qp∨q is also true when ppp and qqq are both true. The sentence “x≥0x\geq 0x≥0 or x≤0x\leq 0x≤0” is true for x=0x=0x=0.

The conditional

§0.1 Propositions and connectives

The conditional is the most important connective for us, because almost every theorem has the form “if hypothesis, then conclusion”. In p⟹qp\entonces qp⟹q, the proposition ppp is the hypothesis (or antecedent) and qqq is the conclusion (or consequent).

The last two rows of the table are surprising at first: when the hypothesis is false, the conditional is true, no matter what the conclusion says. The reason becomes clear with a promise. If I say “if it rains, then I carry an umbrella”, I only break my word when it rains and I do not carry an umbrella. If it does not rain, I keep my promise whether or not I carry an umbrella.

Ways to read p ⟹ q

§0.1 Propositions and connectives
Ways to read p⟹qp\entonces qp⟹q

All of the following sentences mean the same thing:

  • if ppp, then qqq;

  • ppp implies qqq;

  • qqq, if ppp;

  • ppp only if qqq;

  • ppp is a sufficient condition for qqq;

  • qqq is a necessary condition for ppp.

For example, “being a multiple of 444 is a sufficient condition for being even” and “being even is a necessary condition for being a multiple of 444” say the same thing: if an integer is a multiple of 444, then it is even.

Definition 0.1.4 · Converse, inverse and contrapositive

§0.1 Propositions and connectives
Definition 0.1.4 · Converse, inverse and contrapositive

Given the conditional p⟹qp\entonces qp⟹q:

  1. its converse is q⟹pq\entonces pq⟹p;

  2. its inverse is ¬p⟹¬q\no p\entonces\no q¬p⟹¬q;

  3. its contrapositive is ¬q⟹¬p\no q\entonces\no p¬q⟹¬p.

Example 0.1.5

§0.1 Propositions and connectives
Example 0.1.5

Consider the (true) conditional “if an integer is a multiple of 666, then it is even”.

  1. Converse: “if an integer is even, then it is a multiple of 666”. It is false: 444 is even and is not a multiple of 666.

  2. Inverse: “if an integer is not a multiple of 666, then it is odd”. It is also false, because of the same 444.

  3. Contrapositive: “if an integer is odd, then it is not a multiple of 666”. It is true.

The conditional

§0.1 Propositions and connectives

The example is no coincidence. A conditional and its contrapositive always have the same truth value; on the other hand, a conditional can be true while its converse is false. To say this precisely we need the notion of equivalence.

§0.2

Tautologies and equivalences

Definition 0.2.1 · Tautology, contradiction, equivalence

§0.2 Tautologies and equivalences
Definition 0.2.1 · Tautology, contradiction, equivalence

A compound proposition is a tautology if it is true for all truth values of the propositions that form it, and a contradiction if it is false for all of them. Two compound propositions PPP and QQQ are logically equivalent, and we write P≡QP\equiv QP≡Q, if P⟺QP\sii QP⟺Q is a tautology; that is, if they have the same column in the truth table.

Tautologies and equivalences

§0.2 Tautologies and equivalences

The simplest example of a tautology is p∨¬pp\ou\no pp∨¬p (the law of the excluded middle), and the simplest contradiction is p∧¬pp\y\no pp∧¬p (the law of non-contradiction). The proofs by contradiction in the next chapter rest on these two principles.

The negation of an implication is not another implication

§0.2 Tautologies and equivalences
The negation of an implication is not another implication

A very common mistake is to believe that the negation of “if ppp, then qqq” is “if ppp, then not qqq”. It is not: by item 5 of the previous theorem, negating p⇒qp\Rightarrow qp⇒q means asserting that ppp happens and qqq does not. For example, the negation of “if a number is prime, then it is odd” is “there is a prime number that is not odd” (and it is true: 222). To refute an implication it is enough to exhibit such a case: a counterexample.

Rules of inference

A proof advances by applying rules of inference: patterns that let us pass from propositions already established to a new one. The most common are the following; each one corresponds to a tautology.

Example 0.2.3 · A chain of inferences

§0.2 Tautologies and equivalences
Example 0.2.3 · A chain of inferences

Suppose we know that (a) if an integer is a multiple of 666, then it is a multiple of 333; (b) if an integer is a multiple of 333, then the sum of its digits is a multiple of 333; and (c) n=246n=246n=246 is a multiple of 666. By hypothetical syllogism, from (a) and (b): if an integer is a multiple of 666, the sum of its digits is a multiple of 333. By modus ponens with (c): the sum of the digits of 246246246 is a multiple of 333 (indeed, 2+4+6=122+4+6=122+4+6=12).

§0.3

Quantifiers

Quantifiers

§0.3 Quantifiers

Expressions such as “x+1=5x+1=5x+1=5” or “nnn is prime” are not propositions: whether they are true depends on who xxx or nnn is. They are called propositional functions and we write them p(x)p(x)p(x). One way to turn them into propositions is to quantify them.

Definition 0.3.1 · Quantifiers

§0.3 Quantifiers
Definition 0.3.1 · Quantifiers

Let p(x)p(x)p(x) be a propositional function whose variable xxx takes values in a set UUU.

  1. ∀x∈U, p(x)\forall x\in U,\ p(x)∀x∈U, p(x) is read “for all xxx in UUU, p(x)p(x)p(x)”. It is true if p(x)p(x)p(x) is true for every element xxx of UUU. The symbol ∀\forall∀ is the universal quantifier.

  2. ∃x∈U, p(x)\exists x\in U,\ p(x)∃x∈U, p(x) is read “there exists xxx in UUU such that p(x)p(x)p(x)”. It is true if p(x)p(x)p(x) is true for at least one element of UUU. The symbol ∃\exists∃ is the existential quantifier.

  3. ∃! x∈U, p(x)\exists!\, x\in U,\ p(x)∃!x∈U, p(x) is read “there exists a unique xxx in UUU such that p(x)p(x)p(x)”. It means that there is an element satisfying ppp and that any other element satisfying it is equal to it.

Example 0.3.2

§0.3 Quantifiers
Example 0.3.2

With U=RU=\RU=R:

  1. ∀x, x2≥0\forall x,\ x^2\geq 0∀x, x2≥0 is true.

  2. ∀x, x2>0\forall x,\ x^2>0∀x, x2>0 is false: it fails for x=0x=0x=0.

  3. ∃x, x2=2\exists x,\ x^2=2∃x, x2=2 is true (x=2x=\sqrt2x=2​); but if U=QU=\QU=Q it is false, as we will prove in the next chapter. The set UUU matters.

  4. ∃!x, 2x+1=7\exists! x,\ 2x+1=7∃!x, 2x+1=7 is true: x=3x=3x=3 is a solution, and if 2x+1=72x+1=72x+1=7 and 2y+1=72y+1=72y+1=7, subtracting gives x=yx=yx=y.

Quantifiers

§0.3 Quantifiers

Quantifiers appear everywhere, although they are hidden in the language. “Between two distinct real numbers there is another real number” means: ∀a, ∀b, (a<b⇒∃c, a<c<b)\forall a,\ \forall b,\ (a<b\Rightarrow \exists c,\ a<c<b)∀a, ∀b, (a<b⇒∃c, a<c<b). When we study geometry we will see the same thing in sentences like “through two distinct points there passes a unique line”.

Quantifiers

§0.3 Quantifiers

In words: saying that not all satisfy ppp is saying that some do not satisfy it; and saying that none satisfies ppp is saying that all satisfy its negation. To negate a quantified proposition, change each ∀\forall∀ into ∃\exists∃, each ∃\exists∃ into ∀\forall∀, and negate what is left at the end.

The order of the quantifiers matters

§0.3 Quantifiers
The order of the quantifiers matters

Compare (a)  ∀x∈R, ∃y∈R, x+y=0y(b)  ∃y∈R, ∀x∈R, x+y=0.\text{(a)}\ \ \forall x\in\R,\ \exists y\in\R,\ x+y=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \text{(b)}\ \ \exists y\in\R,\ \forall x\in\R,\ x+y=0.(a)  ∀x∈R, ∃y∈R, x+y=0and(b)  ∃y∈R, ∀x∈R, x+y=0. Sets

§0.4 Sets

We take the notion of set as primitive: a set is a collection of objects, its elements. We write a∈Aa\in Aa∈A if aaa is an element of AAA, and a∉Aa\notin Aa∈/A otherwise. A set is determined by its elements, and it is described by listing them, A={1,2,3}A=\{1,2,3\}A={1,2,3}, or by a defining property, A={x∈N:1≤x≤3}A=\{x\in\N : 1\leq x\leq 3\}A={x∈N:1≤x≤3}. The empty set ∅\varnothing∅ has no elements.

We will use the usual number sets: the natural numbers N={1,2,3,…}\N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}N={1,2,3,…}, the integers Z\ZZ, the rationals Q\QQ and the reals R\RR.

Definition 0.4.1 · Inclusion and equality

§0.4 Sets
Definition 0.4.1 · Inclusion and equality

AAA is a subset of BBB, written A⊆BA\subseteq BA⊆B, if every element of AAA is an element of BBB: A⊆B ⟺ ∀x, (x∈A⟹x∈B).A\subseteq B\ \sii\ \forall x,\ (x\in A\entonces x\in B).A⊆B ⟺ ∀x, (x∈A⟹x∈B). Two sets are equal if they have the same elements; equivalently, A=B⟺(A⊆B∧B⊆A)A=B\sii (A\subseteq B\y B\subseteq A)A=B⟺(A⊆B∧B⊆A).

Sets

§0.4 Sets

The last equivalence is the standard way to prove that two sets are equal: the two inclusions are proved separately. We will do this several times in geometry; for example, to prove that the perpendicular bisector of a segment is exactly the set of points equidistant from its endpoints.

Definition 0.4.2 · Operations with sets

Definition 0.4.2 · Operations with sets

Let AAA and BBB be subsets of a universal set U\UU.

  1. Union: A∪B={x:x∈A∨x∈B}A\cup B=\{x: x\in A\ou x\in B\}A∪B={x:x∈A∨x∈B}.

  2. Intersection: A∩B={x:x∈A∧x∈B}A\cap B=\{x: x\in A\y x\in B\}A∩B={x:x∈A∧x∈B}. If A∩B=∅A\cap B=\varnothingA∩B=∅ we say that AAA and BBB are disjoint.

  3. Difference: A∖B={x:x∈A∧x∉B}A\setminus B=\{x: x\in A\y x\notin B\}A∖B={x:x∈A∧x∈/B}.

  4. Complement: Ac=U∖A={x∈U:x∉A}A^{c}=\U\setminus A=\{x\in\U: x\notin A\}Ac=U∖A={x∈U:x∈/A}.

  5. Cartesian product: A×B={(a,b):a∈A∧b∈B}A\times B=\{(a,b): a\in A\y b\in B\}A×B={(a,b):a∈A∧b∈B}.

Equivalence relations

§0.4 Sets

One last notion, which we will use when defining vectors in chapter 11. A relation on a set AAA is a subset R⊆A×A\mathcal R\subseteq A\times AR⊆A×A; we write a R ba\,\mathcal R\,baRb if (a,b)∈R(a,b)\in\mathcal R(a,b)∈R.

Definition 0.4.5 · Equivalence relation

§0.4 Sets
Definition 0.4.5 · Equivalence relation

A relation R\mathcal RR on AAA is an equivalence relation if it is

  1. reflexive: a R aa\,\mathcal R\,aaRa for every a∈Aa\in Aa∈A;

  2. symmetric: if a R ba\,\mathcal R\,baRb, then b R ab\,\mathcal R\,abRa;

  3. transitive: if a R ba\,\mathcal R\,baRb and b R cb\,\mathcal R\,cbRc, then a R ca\,\mathcal R\,caRc.

The equivalence class of aaa is [a]={x∈A:x R a}[a]=\{x\in A: x\,\mathcal R\,a\}[a]={x∈A:xRa}.

Equivalence relations

§0.4 Sets

Equality, the relation “having the same parity” among integers and the relation “being born in the same month” among people are equivalence relations. Two equivalence classes are either equal or disjoint, so an equivalence relation splits the set into “bundles” of elements that it cannot tell apart. Many more will appear in the course: congruence of segments and of triangles, similarity, parallelism; and a vector will be precisely one of those bundles.

What we take away from week 1

Key ideas
  • p→qp\to qp→q is false only when ppp is true and qqq is false.
  • p→qp\to qp→q is equivalent to its contrapositive ¬q→¬p\lnot q\to\lnot p¬q→¬p; the converse is not.
  • ¬(∀x p(x))≡∃x ¬p(x)\lnot(\forall x\,p(x))\equiv\exists x\,\lnot p(x)¬(∀xp(x))≡∃x¬p(x): one counterexample is enough.
